# LanguageLab

Learn a language by using it. A free, private web app with no account, no ads, no streak and no points.

Live at <https://adervec.github.io/LanguageLab/> once the repository is public.

## Why

The maker kept a 1000-day streak on a tile-tapping app and still could not use the language. The
likely reasons: the exercises were recognition and not production, the streak became the goal,
there was far too little to read and hear, and nothing measured what they could actually do.
LanguageLab is built to be the opposite on each count.

## What you do in it

| | |
|---|---|
| **First steps** | From nothing, in 44 languages: ten short lessons, from everyday phrases to first sentences about family, the day, likes, places and the weather. You hear each phrase with its meaning (and a reading in Latin letters where the script is new), type it from the ear with the lesson's letters there to tap, write it back from its meaning, and say it. The phrases then join your review. For 14 scripts there is a chart of the letters to hear and practise; for nineteen languages a list of the sounds that surprise. |
| **A sitting** (about twenty minutes) | Hear a story with the text hidden. Answer three questions from what you heard. Read it and tap every word you do not know. Take dictation at natural speed. Write a sentence back from its meaning. Say sentences aloud. Retell it in your own words. |
| **Speak** | Dialogues you play a part in: your lines read aloud, then said from memory, scored by how much a speech recogniser understood. You can also record yourself and hear your take after the model's. |
| **Conversation** | Talk with an AI partner, by voice or typing. It answers at your level, speaks its reply, and corrects your last message as you go. With the browser's own small model the corrections are offered for you to keep or not, because it is sometimes wrong; an AI agent of your own corrects properly. |
| **Pen pal** | Write a letter. An AI pen pal answers at your level and corrects your mistakes. |
| **Review** | The words you tapped come back as a blank in the sentence you met them in, and you type them. Your corrected mistakes come back until you type them right. Spaced so they return just before you forget. |
| **More input** | Just listen (stories one after another) and free reading, with a tap for the meaning of any word. |
| **Progress** | Which level your numbers support for reading and for listening, a table level by level, and your totals of reading and listening. |
| **With a teacher** | Send a sitting to a teacher or a tutor as a link. They open it in any browser, write their corrections and send a link back, which puts the corrections beside your sitting and into your review. No account and no server: the text travels inside the links. You can also copy a sitting as plain text and type corrections in by hand. |

## Languages

Forty-four languages come with first lessons and with graded stories and dialogues.

French, Spanish, German, Italian, Portuguese, Dutch, Croatian and English have 16 stories and
8 dialogues each (French has more), from A1 to B2. Mandarin Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Swedish have
half that (8 stories and 4 dialogues, A1 to B1); in Chinese and Japanese a tapped word shows its
reading first, and dictation is marked character by character.

Thirty-two more have a first handful, four stories and two dialogues at A1 and A2 (a market, a day, a
weekend by the sea, a new neighbour; a café and a hotel): Russian, Ukrainian, Polish, Czech, Slovak,
Slovenian, Serbian, Bulgarian, Macedonian, Belarusian, Romanian, Hungarian, Greek, Turkish, Norwegian,
Danish, Finnish, Catalan, Galician, Afrikaans, Indonesian, Malay, Vietnamese, Filipino, Swahili,
Esperanto, Arabic, Persian, Hindi, Urdu, Bengali and Thai. Six texts are a start, not a course: after
them the material is your own texts and the stories an AI agent writes to your level.

All 44 have first lessons for complete beginners (ten lessons, eighty phrases and first sentences).
Where the script is not Latin each phrase has a reading in Latin letters, the letters a text uses are
on screen to tap, and there is a chart of the letters to hear and practise. The meaning of a phrase is
shown in the language of the interface where that is one of the seventeen, else in English. Marking
forgives what a keyboard makes hard: accents everywhere, the vowel marks and letter shapes of Arabic,
Persian and Urdu, and the half-space inside a Persian word.

Every other language works with your own texts and with stories written to your level by an AI agent
through the cowork folder.

The starter stories, the dialogues and the first lessons were written by an AI and have not been
checked by native speakers; the app says so in Settings. The interface is in English and 16 other
languages (machine translated, as the app also says).

## The AI parts

LanguageLab has no server and calls no AI service. The AI features (new stories, the review of each
sitting, the pen pal, and the conversation where the browser has no model of its own) work through a
folder you choose: the app writes a request there, any AI agent you run reads it and writes a reply,
and the app shows you the reply to approve (a conversation reply is shown as it comes). See "AI
cowork" in Settings. Everything else works without it.

## Running it

It is a static site with no build step.

```
python -m http.server
```

then open <http://localhost:8000/> in Chrome or Edge. Speech needs a browser with speech synthesis;
speaking practice needs speech recognition (Chrome, Edge, Safari).

## Checks

```
node core.test.mjs
npm i --no-save playwright-core && node .github/smoke.mjs && node .github/flow.mjs && node .github/offline.mjs
```

`core.test.mjs` also checks every bundled story, dialogue and first lesson; `content/AUTHORING.md`
says how to write more. `.github/spell.mjs` checks every word of the texts against open spelling
dictionaries (31 languages; its first lines say how to install them). `flow.mjs` drives the whole app in headless Chrome, including a cowork folder
with replies written as files; `offline.mjs` stops the server after one visit and opens the app again.

## What has been checked, and what has not

- **On every push** (CI): the logic and every bundled text (`core.test.mjs`); the whole app in headless Chrome
  (`flow.mjs`), with stand-ins for the things a test cannot have (a microphone, the on-device translator and model, the
  folder picker); opening without a connection (`offline.mjs`).
- **By hand in a real Chrome (version 157, November 2026)**: a conversation with Chrome's own on-device model, in French
  (replies in one to three seconds; its corrections caught about one mistake a message and were sometimes wrong, which is
  why they are only offered); the French voice reading a story sentence; the speaking step scored by Chrome's real
  recogniser from a recording fed in place of the microphone (the exact sentence: 100%; two words changed: 88%, those two
  marked); the on-device translator for French; and that the recogniser gives three guesses for a line, each with how sure
  it is (0.96, 0.94 and 0.93 for a clear recording), which is what the "nearly right" marks are made from.
- **With a real AI agent** (Claude, November 2026), answering from the instruction files in the folder and nothing else: a
  sitting graded and corrected, a pen-pal letter answered, a two-turn conversation, and five stories and two dialogues in
  Swedish (a language that had no bundled stories then), which passed the same checker as the bundled texts
  and have since joined the app. The replies waited
  for approval across a browser restart and were applied once.
- **The first lessons** (3,520 phrases) were read a second time by their author, which changed six of them, and the
  sixteen languages most likely to hide mistakes were read by a second AI model, which changed two more.
- **Spelling** (`.github/spell.mjs`, November 2026): every word of every story, dialogue, question and first lesson in the
  31 languages that have an open spelling dictionary, 21,648 different word forms. 169 were not in a dictionary. Each was
  read: one colloquial word was replaced with the standard one, and the other 168 were names, loanwords, regional words
  or correct forms the dictionary lacks (they are listed in `.github/spell-ok.json`). A spelling dictionary knows no
  grammar, and there is none for Chinese, Japanese, Finnish, Afrikaans, Indonesian, Malay, Filipino, Swahili, Arabic,
  Hindi, Urdu, Bengali or Thai.
- **A second reader** for the 192 texts in the 32 newer languages: another AI model (Claude Sonnet) read each one with
  its glosses, questions and notes and marked eight things. Six were changed (a Macedonian greeting, also in the first
  lessons, and a word order; an Afrikaans question; a Vietnamese and a Swahili phrase); two were judged right as
  written. It reported nothing in 27 of the languages, which is weaker evidence than a native reader finding nothing.
- **Not checked**: a physical microphone, how the voices sound to a native ear, Google sign-in, and phones. The texts and phrases were written by an AI and no native speaker
  has read them.
