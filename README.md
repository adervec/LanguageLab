# LanguageLab

Learn a language by using it. A free, private web app with no account, no ads, no streak and no points.

Live at <https://adervec.github.io/LanguageLab/> once the repository is public.

## Why

The maker kept a 1000-day streak on a tile-tapping app and still could not use the language. The
likely reasons: the exercises were recognition and not production, the streak became the goal,
there was far too little to read and hear, and nothing measured what they could actually do.
LanguageLab is built to be the opposite on each count.

## What you do in it

| | |
|---|---|
| **First steps** | From nothing, in 44 languages: thirty short lessons, from everyday phrases to first sentences about family, the day, likes, places and the weather, then shopping, a restaurant, free time, reasons and rules, and two lessons that are each a small story. Thirty-two languages have thirty more (French, Spanish, German, Italian, Portuguese, Dutch, Croatian, English, Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, Indonesian, Malay, Russian, Ukrainian, Bulgarian, Polish, Czech, Slovak, Serbian, Romanian, Hungarian, Greek, Turkish, Catalan, Galician, Swedish, Norwegian, Danish, Afrikaans and Esperanto), each a small scene: a conversation at the doctor's, the station or the bank, or a story of eight sentences. You hear each phrase with its meaning (and a reading in Latin letters where the script is new), type it from the ear with the lesson's letters there to tap, write it back from its meaning, and say it. The phrases then join your review, and Find a phrase searches them all by a word of either language, as a phrasebook to listen to. For 14 scripts there is a chart of the letters to hear and practise; for every language a list of the sounds that surprise, each with a word to hear and to say. Numbers by ear: your device's voice says a number at natural speed and you type it in digits, ten at a time, in four ranges up to 10,000. Sounds apart, in 24 languages: pairs of words one sound apart ("dessus" and "dessous"), first by ear (which of the two was said?), then aloud (which of the two did the browser hear?). With an AI agent of your own (AI cowork), further lessons are written on request and wait for your approval, also for a language the app brings no lessons for. |
| **A sitting** (about twenty minutes) | Hear a story with the text hidden. Answer three questions from what you heard. Read it and tap every word you do not know. Take dictation at natural speed. Write a sentence back from its meaning. Say sentences aloud. Retell it in your own words. |
| **Speak** | Dialogues you play a part in: your lines read aloud, then said from memory, scored by how much a speech recogniser understood. Any story, and any text of your own, can be read aloud sentence by sentence and scored the same way. Where it hears another word in place of yours, it shows the two side by side with the letters they do not share marked. In First steps each sound to notice has a word to say on its own. On a computer it also listens to the sound itself: about how long you took over a line, set beside the model voice's own time, and your long pauses; and in Chinese, Thai and Vietnamese, how your pitch moved on a word said on its own, drawn and named. In eight languages that have such a pair (French u and ou, German ü and u, and the like) it measures a held vowel itself and says whether the tongue was forward, back or in between. Every word said on its own in First steps is counted: Progress shows how many of them speech recognition understood and which sounds it understands least from you, and those words go to your AI agent with the request for new dialogues and lessons, so that the sounds come back inside sentences you say. You can also record yourself and hear your take after the model's. |
| **Conversation** | Talk with an AI partner, by voice or typing. It answers at your level, speaks its reply, and corrects your last message as you go. With the browser's own small model the corrections are offered for you to keep or not, because it is sometimes wrong; an AI agent of your own corrects properly. |
| **Pen pal** | Write a letter. An AI pen pal answers at your level and corrects your mistakes. |
| **Review** | The words you tapped come back as a blank in the sentence you met them in, and you type them. Your corrected mistakes come back until you type them right. Spaced so they return just before you forget. |
| **More input** | Just listen (the stories you have read and the first lessons you have done, one after another) and free reading, with a tap for the meaning of any word. Where the browser has its own AI model, "Write me a story" adds a new short text at your level in a few seconds, written on your device around the words you are reviewing (in Chrome 157: French, Spanish, German, English and Japanese). It is a small model's writing, with about one slip a text, and the app says so. |
| **Progress** | Which level your numbers support for reading, for listening and for speaking, a table level by level, your totals of reading and listening, and where each sound stands. If you are not starting from nothing, Find my level by ear plays two sentences at each level at natural speed and says where it gets hard: that is where you begin. |
| **With a teacher** | Send a sitting to a teacher or a tutor as a link. They open it in any browser, write their corrections and send a link back, which puts the corrections beside your sitting and into your review. No account and no server: the text travels inside the links. You can also copy a sitting as plain text and type corrections in by hand. |
| **With a native speaker** | The lessons and texts were written by an AI. Settings gives you a link for someone whose own language it is: on their page they read as much as they like, lesson by lesson and text by text, write how a line should read where it is wrong or not what people say, tick what they have read, and send the result back to you as text. No account and no server. Nobody has done this for any language yet. |

## Languages

Forty-four languages come with first lessons and with graded stories and dialogues.

French, Spanish, German, Italian, Portuguese, Dutch, Croatian and English have 16 stories and
8 dialogues each (French has more), from A1 to B2.

The other 36 have half that: 8 stories and 4 dialogues, from A1 to B1. Mandarin Chinese, Japanese,
Korean and Swedish have sets of their own; in Chinese and Japanese a tapped word shows its reading
first, and dictation is marked character by character. Thirty-two share one set of twelve texts, the
same in each language (a market, a day, a family, a town, a weekend by the sea, a new neighbour, a
birthday, a lost wallet; a café, a hotel, a ticket office and a doctor's): Russian, Ukrainian, Polish,
Czech, Slovak, Slovenian, Serbian, Bulgarian, Macedonian, Belarusian, Romanian, Hungarian, Greek,
Turkish, Norwegian, Danish, Finnish, Catalan, Galician, Afrikaans, Indonesian, Malay, Vietnamese,
Filipino, Swahili, Esperanto, Arabic, Persian, Hindi, Urdu, Bengali and Thai. Twelve texts are a
start, not a course: after them the material is your own texts and the stories an AI agent writes to
your level.

All 44 have twelve lessons of words with pictures (96 everyday nouns: food, animals, the house, clothes, the body, the town; with the article in the languages where that is how the gender shows). All 44 have first lessons for complete beginners (thirty lessons, 240 phrases and first sentences; sixty lessons in the eight with full sets and in 30 more: Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, Indonesian, Malay, Russian, Ukrainian, Bulgarian, Polish, Czech, Slovak, Serbian, Romanian, Hungarian, Greek, Turkish, Catalan, Galician, Swedish, Norwegian, Danish, Afrikaans, Esperanto, Arabic, Hindi, Persian, Urdu, Bengali and Swahili).
Where the script is not Latin each phrase has a reading in Latin letters, the letters a text uses are
on screen to tap, and there is a chart of the letters to hear and practise. The meaning of a phrase is
shown in the language of the interface where that is one of the seventeen, else in English. Marking
forgives what a keyboard makes hard: accents everywhere, the vowel marks and letter shapes of Arabic,
Persian and Urdu, and the half-space inside a Persian word.

Every other language works with your own texts and with stories written to your level by an AI agent
through the cowork folder.

The starter stories, the dialogues and the first lessons were written by an AI and have not been
checked by native speakers; the app says so in Settings, and gives a link there to a page on which a native
speaker can read all of it and write what is wrong. The interface is in English and 16 other
languages (machine translated, as the app also says).

## The AI parts

LanguageLab has no server and calls no AI service. The AI features (new stories, the review of each
sitting, the pen pal, and the conversation where the browser has no model of its own) work through a
folder you choose: the app writes a request there, any AI agent you run reads it and writes a reply,
and the app shows you the reply to approve (a conversation reply is shown as it comes). See "AI
cowork" in Settings. Everything else works without it.

Where the browser carries a small AI model of its own, two things also work with no folder and no agent: the
conversation, and a new short text to read and hear ("Write me a story" in the Library). Nothing leaves the device.
Chrome 157's model writes French, Spanish, German, English and Japanese; for the other 39 languages these two stay with
your agent.

## Running it

It is a static site with no build step.

```
python -m http.server
```

then open <http://localhost:8000/> in Chrome or Edge. Speech needs a browser with speech synthesis;
speaking practice needs speech recognition (Chrome, Edge, Safari).

## Checks

```
node core.test.mjs
npm i --no-save playwright-core && node .github/smoke.mjs && node .github/flow.mjs && node .github/offline.mjs
```

`core.test.mjs` also checks every bundled story, dialogue and first lesson; `content/AUTHORING.md`
says how to write more. `.github/spell.mjs` checks every word of the texts against open spelling
dictionaries (31 languages; its first lines say how to install them). `flow.mjs` drives the whole app in headless Chrome, including a cowork folder
with replies written as files; `offline.mjs` stops the server after one visit and opens the app again.

## What has been checked, and what has not

- **On every push** (CI): the logic and every bundled text (`core.test.mjs`); the whole app in headless Chrome
  (`flow.mjs`), with stand-ins for the things a test cannot have (a microphone, the on-device translator and model, the
  folder picker); opening without a connection (`offline.mjs`).
- **By hand in a real Chrome (version 157, November 2026)**: a conversation with Chrome's own on-device model, in French
  (replies in one to three seconds; its corrections caught about one mistake a message and were sometimes wrong, which is
  why they are only offered); the French voice reading a story sentence; the speaking step scored by Chrome's real
  recogniser from a recording fed in place of the microphone (the exact sentence: 100%; two words changed: 88%, those two
  marked); the on-device translator for French; and that the recogniser gives three guesses for a line, each with how sure
  it is (0.96, 0.94 and 0.93 for a clear recording), which is what the "nearly right" marks are made from.
- **A text written on the device** (Chrome 157's own model, November 2026): the model offers itself for five of the 44
  languages (French, Spanish, German, English, Japanese). In the app, in French, a tap added a text of about 90 words in four
  to five seconds (up to twenty the first time after the page opens); it opened for reading and listening like any other.
  Eighteen texts were read, some only in part, while the request was being worded. Asked for "a story", the French ones came in the literary past
  tense whatever the instruction said; asked for a message to a friend they come in the tenses people speak in, so that is
  what the app asks for. What was left: about one slip a text in French and Spanish (a word forced in where it does not
  fit, a missing preposition), two or three in German (a case ending, a verb wrongly made reflexive), an awkward sentence
  in Japanese. That is why these texts carry no level, never count towards what Progress says you can do, and are
  labelled as written on the device.
- **A retelling read on the device** (the same model): a French retelling with three mistakes planted in it. Sixteen
  seconds after the sitting ended the app showed two of them corrected rightly, one with the wrong reason given; the third
  was missed. That is why these are shown as suggestions to keep or leave, and never as a grade.
- **A sound said on its own** (Chrome 157's recogniser, November 2026), with recordings of single words played in place
  of the microphone. They were made with this computer's English voice, there being no French one, so they are a test
  of the app and not of anyone's French. "Moi" was understood, and the row said so. A word meant as "hôtel" was heard as
  "Ciao" and one played against "rue" as "chat", and each was shown beside the word wanted with the letters marked. A
  fourth was not heard at all, and the app said that nothing was heard.
- **With a real AI agent** (Claude, November 2026), answering from the instruction files in the folder and nothing else: a
  sitting graded and corrected, a pen-pal letter answered, a two-turn conversation, and five stories and two dialogues in
  Swedish (a language that had no bundled stories then), which passed the same checker as the bundled texts
  and have since joined the app. The replies waited
  for approval across a browser restart and were applied once. For first lessons the same agent answered a request for
  Hebrew, for which the app brings nothing, with three lessons of eight phrases: all 24 passed the checks a reply
  goes through (short, no digits, said once, markable, with a reading). Nobody who speaks Hebrew has read them.
- **Words with pictures** (November 2026): 4,224 words, 96 in each of 44 languages, written directly by the AI. The test checks what can be
  checked by rule: 96 words and 96 pictures, a reading in Latin letters where the script is not Latin, and no word
  twice in a language (which is how Korean 눈, snow and eye, Indonesian anggur, grapes and wine, Swahili ndege, bird
  and plane, and the Dutch and Afrikaans bank, sofa and bank, were caught and one of each said another way). The
  spelling dictionaries cover 31 of the languages. A few choices are loans or the less usual of two words (Filipino
  "fountain", Swahili "eropleni" for the plane). The pictures are emoji: they look different on each device, and an
  old device shows an empty box for some. Tried in headless Chrome only.
  A larger model then read all of them, five readers by family of languages, each copy with one mistake planted per
  language (a wrong gender, a missing diacritic, a neighbour's word): 42 of the 44 were found (missed: an Esperanto ĉ
  without its circumflex, and a Russian soft sign on a Bulgarian word), with eleven real remarks, of which eight were
  taken (Galician "as gafas", Swedish "en tv", Afrikaans "handsak", Malay "baju-T", Filipino "hotel", Hindi
  "चिड़िया" for the bird and a nukta, and the Arabic قميص now said to mean a shirt) and three left as written
  (Dutch "de zitbank", because "de bank" is also the bank; the formal Hungarian word for a motorbike; a Bengali
  reading that was right). Nouns are the kind of content such a read checks best; it is still not a person.
- **The first lessons** (19,680 phrases: 240 in each of 44 languages, and 240 more in 38 of them). Lessons 1 to 10 were read a second time by their
  author, which changed six phrases. A second AI model then read them in three rounds that between them cover every
  phrase in every language but English (whose phrases are the English meanings themselves). Lessons 1 to 10 in the
  sixteen languages most likely to hide mistakes: two phrases changed. Lessons 11 to 20 in 32 languages: 15 things
  marked, 12 changed or given a note (Afrikaans word order and the "om" before a time, also in one story; a Finnish
  form; a clearer Greek word; two Filipino phrases), three judged right as written. The remaining 3,200 phrases, in 32
  languages: two things marked, one changed (a Korean ending) and one judged right as written. Lessons 21 to 30 (3,440 phrases in the 43 languages
  other than English) were read in four parts, each copy with three plain mistakes planted. All twelve were found, and
  six other things were marked. Two were changed (a Slovene verb that needed the form for a habit; the Belarusian for
  "each other"), one was reworded for another reason (the Serbian for "she will call"), and three were judged right as
  written (an Esperanto ending, an Afrikaans word, a Swahili phrase whose own meaning the reader overlooked). The
  Slovene slip was in the Slovak too, where no reader marked it: it was found by looking for it. Each reader took
  about twenty seconds over some 900 phrases, so this is a fast read that catches plain mistakes and little more. Lessons 31 to 60 (1,680 phrases in the seven
  languages other than English) were read in two parts with seven plain mistakes planted: all seven were found, and
  three other things were marked. Two were changed (the Dutch for "for six months now"; a Croatian phrase that also
  read "a problem with myself") and one was judged right as written (the Croatian word for a bank's receipt). A
  second look by their writer changed four more (three Italian phrases, one Dutch). Lessons 31 to 60 in Chinese,
  Japanese, Korean, Russian, Polish and Swedish (1,440 phrases, with a reading in Latin letters in the first four)
  were each read by a larger model, one language a reader, in a copy with three subtle mistakes planted (an
  agreement or a particle, a number that no longer matched the meaning, a wrong syllable in a reading): all eighteen
  were found. Six other things were marked and all six changed: a Swedish verb that wants "att", a Japanese phrase no
  speaker would say, three Korean ones (a storm that "was", a misspelt ending, a road that was jammed where it should
  be closed), and "the third floor", which in Japanese, Chinese, Korean and Russian counts the ground floor as the
  first and now says so. The Russian, Polish and Chinese readers marked nothing but the planted mistakes. The same
  was then done for Ukrainian, Czech, Turkish, Greek, Danish and Norwegian (1,440 phrases): all eighteen planted
  mistakes were found, and nine other things were marked and changed (two Ukrainian wordings, three Czech, two

  mistakes. Each reader took some twenty seconds over its 240 phrases, so a clean report is worth what finding

  changed (the Romanian for a flat battery, a Catalan tense, a Bulgarian verb). The one missed was a wrong Slovak

- **The sound itself** (Chrome 157, November 2026), with sounds played in place of the microphone. A recording of one

  sentence, "the voice takes 1.9". Four glides of a plain tone played to the listener on the four Chinese tone rows
  (rising from 150 to 300 Hz, falling, down and up again, steady): each was named and drawn rightly, while the
  recogniser made no word of them. A glide is not a voice: nobody has spoken a tone into it, and a noisy room, a
  creaky voice or a second voice will mislead it. It listens only in Chrome and browsers built on it, on a computer, where it was tried and where two listeners
  may share a microphone; the page says so where it does; nothing of the sound is kept or sent, only those numbers in the page.
- **Sounds apart** (November 2026): the 174 pairs were read by a second model in a copy with six plain mistakes
  planted (wrong meanings, a pair two sounds apart, a wrong tone). It found all six and marked two more. One was right
  (a Chinese pair that differs in two tones) and was replaced; one was wrong (it took the French "chevaux" for
  "cheval"). A second look by their writer replaced three more that are more than one sound apart in some standard
  pronunciations (two Italian, one Turkish). With Chrome 157's French voice the drill said "blanc", took the right tap
  and the wrong one, and showed what each word means. The half that is said aloud has only been driven by the flow
  test's stand-in recogniser: a single word is hard for a real one, and a word with a homophone can come back
  spelt as the other word.
- **Two more reads of the sixty lessons in the eight main languages** (November 2026), both by a larger AI model, neither by a
  person. The first asked whether anything was wrong, with three mistakes planted in each copy (French, Spanish,
  German, Italian; one of the three a wrong preposition, the kind such a reader had missed before): all twelve were
  found, and one real thing, the accent on the Spanish name María. The second asked a different question, one
  language a reader: for each lesson, which phrase would a native speaker most object to, and is it wrong,
  unnatural or fine? That brought back 160 phrases (French 21, Spanish 25, German 16, Italian 8, Portuguese 24,
  Dutch 15, Croatian 18, English 33), of which 149 were taken and 11 judged right as written. Nearly all were correct
  but stiff or translated: "Cela dépend" where people say "Ça dépend", "Vendez-vous" for "Est-ce que vous vendez",
  a German "Es ist" for "Das ist", "¡Buen provecho!" for the "¡Que aproveche!" of Spain, "met de kaart betalen" for
  "pinnen", an English "At what time". A few were more than that: a Brazilian lamp that was a light bulb, a Croatian
  pronoun out of its place, an Italian idiom with the wrong meaning beside it. 27 of the English meanings shown to
  every learner were made plainer at the same time. What this shows about the rest is not flattering: a read that
  only asks "is it wrong?" had passed all of these, and the other 36 languages have had only that kind of read.
  A learner who had already done one of these lessons finds the corrected wording in review; the card keeps its
  schedule.
  Russian, Japanese, Chinese and Korean were asked the same question next, and it marked more there: 20, 32, 42 and
  50 phrases of 480. 124 were reworded (17, 31, 39 and 37), each with its reading, and 15 readings corrected. Most
  were English shapes in another language: a Japanese 彼女 for one's own mother, a Chinese 是 for a plain yes and a
  table "for two people" where one says how many one is, a Korean 그들 and 그녀 that nobody says aloud, a Russian
  river that "is" behind a house. Fourteen Korean readings wrote kh where a stop meets ㅎ and one hears k. Ukrainian,
  Polish, Arabic and Hindi followed: 6 and 17 of 480, 10 and 23 of 240, and 52 phrases reworded (all 6, 15, all 10,
  21). In Hindi the name Anna had been spelt आना, which reads as the verb "to come"; it is अन्ना now. Then the
  four other languages whose phrases are also shown as meanings to learners who use that interface: Indonesian 28
  and Esperanto 13 of 480, Persian 10 and Urdu 20 of 240; 70 reworded. The Indonesian had "tolong" tacked on
  wherever English says please, the Urdu did the same with براہِ مہربانی, and both asked for the toilet in a word
  from signs. Bengali and Swahili were asked next: 26 and 18 of 240, and 42 reworded
  (all 26, and 16). The Bengali ordered a coffee with a tacked-on দয়া করে where a polite verb does the work, asked
  for the toilet in a word from signs, said goodbye as if for ever (বিদায়) and spoke of one's own mother with the
  written তিনি; the Swahili said "mkahawa" for a restaurant where Tanzania says "mgahawa" (changed in three stories
  too), introduced a mother with "yeye" and shouted the noun "msaada" for help. So the 22 languages read this way
  came out between 6 and 50 remarks each, the fewest where the first writing was best, and the 22 not yet read this
  way should be expected to hold as many. Reading for this also showed that "the second floor" of lesson 12 is one
  floor lower in Finnish, Belarusian, Thai, Filipino and Bengali than a British reader takes it to be; the meaning
  now says so there, as it did in nine languages already.

  object to?) marked 13 sentences, none of them wrong; 12 were reworded ("perdre la voix", a pronoun that pointed

- **The page for a native speaker** (November 2026) was tried in headless Chrome only: the French page (60 lessons, the
  sounds, 44 texts), the Arabic one (right to left, with readings) and the Japanese one opened; a correction and a
  note typed, a section ticked, the result copied (it names each line by where it lives and gives the words it was
  about), all of it still there after a reload; a link naming no language says so. No native speaker has used it,
  so what it is like to read 105 sections on it is not known, and nothing has been corrected through it yet. The
  corrections to lesson phrases and to the words are applied with `node .github/fix-lessons.mjs <their file>` (it
  refuses one whose line has changed since, lists the readings to rewrite and what is left to do by hand); the
  texts are corrected by hand (`content/AUTHORING.md`).
- **Tongue forward or back** (November 2026), the one thing judged from the sound itself. What it measures is where the
  strongest stretch of a held vowel's spectrum lies between 600 and 3,600 Hz, which is about where the second
  resonance of the mouth is: low with the tongue back, high with it forward. It was tried in Chrome with the app's
  real listener, the microphone replaced by a stream playing a sound, never by a person holding a vowel:
  - the two English voices of the maker's computer: a held "oo" read 860 and 750 Hz (back), the half-fronted vowel
    of "food" 1,240 and 1,570 (in between, the second only just), "ee" 2,440 and 3,030 (forward);
  - synthetic vowels with textbook resonances: ou 720 and 820, a half-fronted one 1,390, the French u 1,760 for a
    man's resonances and 2,000 for a woman's, i 2,260; silence gave no answer.
  No voice that says a French u was at hand, so where a person's u falls is taken from the synthetic one. A first
  version measured the centre of gravity of the same band: it read 300 Hz lower when the first resonance tailed off
  slowly and took a u for something halfway to ou, which is why it was replaced; the test keeps a vowel made that
  way. It cannot hear lip rounding, so it does not tell u from i; a noisy room or a breathy voice may mislead it;
  the limits (back below 1,150, forward from 1,600) are a judgement. The page says it is a hint.
- **Find my level by ear** (November 2026): the choice of sentences and the rule (start at the first level where less
  than 80% of the words were caught) are covered by the logic test; the flow test takes the check with a stand-in
  voice, typing the two A1 sentences as given and missing the two of A2, and is told to start at A2. In Chrome 157, with
  its own French voice, the first sentence was spoken on the tap and each Next spoke the following one, and the same
  answers gave the same result. Nobody has taken it by ear: the sentences were typed from the page, not from
  listening. It is two sentences a level, heard cold, so it is rough and errs low, and the page says so; it
  is offered only where the device has a voice for the language and the language has bundled stories.
- **The record of each sound** (November 2026): the counting is covered by the logic test (a try by ear; a try aloud
  understood, nearly, taken for the other word of its pair, or missed; what a merge of two devices keeps) and by the
  flow test, which says words through a stand-in recogniser and reads the counts back in First steps and on Progress.
  No real voice has filled it. It counts one word per sound as the browser's recogniser heard it: a recogniser leans
  towards the words it expects, so a low count can be its fault and a high one is not proof of a good accent.
- **Numbers by ear** (Chrome 157, November 2026), with Chrome's own French voice: the number was spoken (about a
  second of speech for 18), the right digits were accepted, a wrong answer was shown the number, and the next number
  was spoken on its own. What is said for a bare number is the voice's own reading of it: nobody has listened to it,
  in French or in the other 43 languages.
- **Spelling** (`.github/spell.mjs`, November 2026): every word of every story, dialogue, question and first lesson in the
  31 languages that have an open spelling dictionary, 41,750 different word forms. 354 were not in a dictionary. Each was
  read: eight were replaced with the standard form (a colloquial word, five Belarusian words, and Wi-Fi as Russian and Polish write it), and the other 346 were
  names, loanwords, regional words or correct forms the dictionary lacks (they are listed in `.github/spell-ok.json`). A spelling dictionary knows no
  grammar, and there is none for Chinese, Japanese, Finnish, Afrikaans, Indonesian, Malay, Filipino, Swahili, Arabic,
  Hindi, Urdu, Bengali or Thai.
