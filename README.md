# LanguageLab

Learn a language by using it. A free, private web app with no account, no ads, no streak and no points.

Live at <https://adervec.github.io/LanguageLab/> once the repository is public.

## Why

The maker kept a 1000-day streak on a tile-tapping app and still could not use the language. The
likely reasons: the exercises were recognition and not production, the streak became the goal,
there was far too little to read and hear, and nothing measured what they could actually do.
LanguageLab is built to be the opposite on each count.

## What you do in it

| | |
|---|---|
| **First steps** | From nothing, in 44 languages: twenty short lessons, from everyday phrases to first sentences about family, the day, likes, places and the weather. You hear each phrase with its meaning (and a reading in Latin letters where the script is new), type it from the ear with the lesson's letters there to tap, write it back from its meaning, and say it. The phrases then join your review. For 14 scripts there is a chart of the letters to hear and practise; for nineteen languages a list of the sounds that surprise. |
| **A sitting** (about twenty minutes) | Hear a story with the text hidden. Answer three questions from what you heard. Read it and tap every word you do not know. Take dictation at natural speed. Write a sentence back from its meaning. Say sentences aloud. Retell it in your own words. |
| **Speak** | Dialogues you play a part in: your lines read aloud, then said from memory, scored by how much a speech recogniser understood. Any story, and any text of your own, can be read aloud sentence by sentence and scored the same way. You can also record yourself and hear your take after the model's. |
| **Conversation** | Talk with an AI partner, by voice or typing. It answers at your level, speaks its reply, and corrects your last message as you go. With the browser's own small model the corrections are offered for you to keep or not, because it is sometimes wrong; an AI agent of your own corrects properly. |
| **Pen pal** | Write a letter. An AI pen pal answers at your level and corrects your mistakes. |
| **Review** | The words you tapped come back as a blank in the sentence you met them in, and you type them. Your corrected mistakes come back until you type them right. Spaced so they return just before you forget. |
| **More input** | Just listen (stories one after another) and free reading, with a tap for the meaning of any word. Where the browser has its own AI model, "Write me a story" adds a new short text at your level in a few seconds, written on your device around the words you are reviewing (in Chrome 157: French, Spanish, German, English and Japanese). It is a small model's writing, with about one slip a text, and the app says so. |
| **Progress** | Which level your numbers support for reading and for listening, a table level by level, and your totals of reading and listening. |
| **With a teacher** | Send a sitting to a teacher or a tutor as a link. They open it in any browser, write their corrections and send a link back, which puts the corrections beside your sitting and into your review. No account and no server: the text travels inside the links. You can also copy a sitting as plain text and type corrections in by hand. |

## Languages

Forty-four languages come with first lessons and with graded stories and dialogues.

French, Spanish, German, Italian, Portuguese, Dutch, Croatian and English have 16 stories and
8 dialogues each (French has more), from A1 to B2.

The other 36 have half that: 8 stories and 4 dialogues, from A1 to B1. Mandarin Chinese, Japanese,
Korean and Swedish have sets of their own; in Chinese and Japanese a tapped word shows its reading
first, and dictation is marked character by character. Thirty-two share one set of twelve texts, the
same in each language (a market, a day, a family, a town, a weekend by the sea, a new neighbour, a
birthday, a lost wallet; a café, a hotel, a ticket office and a doctor's): Russian, Ukrainian, Polish,
Czech, Slovak, Slovenian, Serbian, Bulgarian, Macedonian, Belarusian, Romanian, Hungarian, Greek,
Turkish, Norwegian, Danish, Finnish, Catalan, Galician, Afrikaans, Indonesian, Malay, Vietnamese,
Filipino, Swahili, Esperanto, Arabic, Persian, Hindi, Urdu, Bengali and Thai. Twelve texts are a
start, not a course: after them the material is your own texts and the stories an AI agent writes to
your level.

All 44 have first lessons for complete beginners (twenty lessons, 160 phrases and first sentences).
Where the script is not Latin each phrase has a reading in Latin letters, the letters a text uses are
on screen to tap, and there is a chart of the letters to hear and practise. The meaning of a phrase is
shown in the language of the interface where that is one of the seventeen, else in English. Marking
forgives what a keyboard makes hard: accents everywhere, the vowel marks and letter shapes of Arabic,
Persian and Urdu, and the half-space inside a Persian word.

Every other language works with your own texts and with stories written to your level by an AI agent
through the cowork folder.

The starter stories, the dialogues and the first lessons were written by an AI and have not been
checked by native speakers; the app says so in Settings. The interface is in English and 16 other
languages (machine translated, as the app also says).

## The AI parts

LanguageLab has no server and calls no AI service. The AI features (new stories, the review of each
sitting, the pen pal, and the conversation where the browser has no model of its own) work through a
folder you choose: the app writes a request there, any AI agent you run reads it and writes a reply,
and the app shows you the reply to approve (a conversation reply is shown as it comes). See "AI
cowork" in Settings. Everything else works without it.

Where the browser carries a small AI model of its own, two things also work with no folder and no agent: the
conversation, and a new short text to read and hear ("Write me a story" in the Library). Nothing leaves the device.
Chrome 157's model writes French, Spanish, German, English and Japanese; for the other 39 languages these two stay with
your agent.

## Running it

It is a static site with no build step.

```
python -m http.server
```

then open <http://localhost:8000/> in Chrome or Edge. Speech needs a browser with speech synthesis;
speaking practice needs speech recognition (Chrome, Edge, Safari).

## Checks

```
node core.test.mjs
npm i --no-save playwright-core && node .github/smoke.mjs && node .github/flow.mjs && node .github/offline.mjs
```

`core.test.mjs` also checks every bundled story, dialogue and first lesson; `content/AUTHORING.md`
says how to write more. `.github/spell.mjs` checks every word of the texts against open spelling
dictionaries (31 languages; its first lines say how to install them). `flow.mjs` drives the whole app in headless Chrome, including a cowork folder
with replies written as files; `offline.mjs` stops the server after one visit and opens the app again.

## What has been checked, and what has not

- **On every push** (CI): the logic and every bundled text (`core.test.mjs`); the whole app in headless Chrome
  (`flow.mjs`), with stand-ins for the things a test cannot have (a microphone, the on-device translator and model, the
  folder picker); opening without a connection (`offline.mjs`).
- **By hand in a real Chrome (version 157, November 2026)**: a conversation with Chrome's own on-device model, in French
  (replies in one to three seconds; its corrections caught about one mistake a message and were sometimes wrong, which is
  why they are only offered); the French voice reading a story sentence; the speaking step scored by Chrome's real
  recogniser from a recording fed in place of the microphone (the exact sentence: 100%; two words changed: 88%, those two
  marked); the on-device translator for French; and that the recogniser gives three guesses for a line, each with how sure
  it is (0.96, 0.94 and 0.93 for a clear recording), which is what the "nearly right" marks are made from.
- **A text written on the device** (Chrome 157's own model, November 2026): the model offers itself for five of the 44
  languages (French, Spanish, German, English, Japanese). In the app, in French, a tap added a text of about 90 words in four
  to five seconds (up to twenty the first time after the page opens); it opened for reading and listening like any other.
  Eighteen texts were read, some only in part, while the request was being worded. Asked for "a story", the French ones came in the literary past
  tense whatever the instruction said; asked for a message to a friend they come in the tenses people speak in, so that is
  what the app asks for. What was left: about one slip a text in French and Spanish (a word forced in where it does not
  fit, a missing preposition), two or three in German (a case ending, a verb wrongly made reflexive), an awkward sentence
  in Japanese. That is why these texts carry no level, never count towards what Progress says you can do, and are
  labelled as written on the device.
- **A retelling read on the device** (the same model): a French retelling with three mistakes planted in it. Sixteen
  seconds after the sitting ended the app showed two of them corrected rightly, one with the wrong reason given; the third
  was missed. That is why these are shown as suggestions to keep or leave, and never as a grade.
- **With a real AI agent** (Claude, November 2026), answering from the instruction files in the folder and nothing else: a
  sitting graded and corrected, a pen-pal letter answered, a two-turn conversation, and five stories and two dialogues in
  Swedish (a language that had no bundled stories then), which passed the same checker as the bundled texts
  and have since joined the app. The replies waited
  for approval across a browser restart and were applied once.
- **The first lessons** (7,040 phrases, 160 in each of 44 languages). Lessons 1 to 10 were read a second time by their
  author, which changed six phrases. A second AI model then read them in three rounds that between them cover every
  phrase in every language but English (whose phrases are the English meanings themselves). Lessons 1 to 10 in the
  sixteen languages most likely to hide mistakes: two phrases changed. Lessons 11 to 20 in 32 languages: 15 things
  marked, 12 changed or given a note (Afrikaans word order and the "om" before a time, also in one story; a Finnish
  form; a clearer Greek word; two Filipino phrases), three judged right as written. The remaining 3,200 phrases, in 32
  languages: two things marked, one changed (a Korean ending) and one judged right as written.
- **What a clean report from that reader is worth**: one of its inputs (880 phrases in thirteen languages) was copied
  with ten mistakes planted in it: a Czech word in Slovak, wrong cases in Slovak, Slovenian and Finnish, a wrong
  gender in Swedish, a wrong preposition and a wrong agreement in Romanian, two Danish meanings swapped and a third
  replaced, a Vietnamese word without its marks. A fresh reader found all ten and marked nothing else. Planted
  mistakes are plain ones: this says nothing about how many subtle ones pass, and nothing about the languages it was
  not tried in.
- **Spelling** (`.github/spell.mjs`, November 2026): every word of every story, dialogue, question and first lesson in the
  31 languages that have an open spelling dictionary, 29,160 different word forms. 230 were not in a dictionary. Each was
  read: three were replaced with the standard form (a colloquial word and two Belarusian words), and the other 227 were
  names, loanwords, regional words or correct forms the dictionary lacks (they are listed in `.github/spell-ok.json`). A spelling dictionary knows no
  grammar, and there is none for Chinese, Japanese, Finnish, Afrikaans, Indonesian, Malay, Filipino, Swahili, Arabic,
  Hindi, Urdu, Bengali or Thai.
- **A second reader** for every bundled text, all 644: another AI model (Claude Sonnet) read each one with its glosses,
  questions and notes, and was told to mark only what it was confident was wrong.
  In the twelve older sets (260 texts, French among them) it marked 22 things. Nineteen were changed: twelve are notes
  added to dialogues where a line the learner speaks depends on whether a man or a woman says it (Portuguese
  "obrigado" and "obrigada", Croatian past forms), and a search for the same thing found one more in Italian; the rest
  are a Spanish meaning given in the wrong tense, a Dutch preposition, a Croatian reflexive, a Japanese reading and a
  Swedish note. Three were judged right as written. It marked nothing in French, German, Italian, Chinese or Korean.
  In the first six texts of each of the 32 newer languages it marked eight things: six were changed (a
  Macedonian greeting, also in the first lessons, and a word order; an Afrikaans question; a Vietnamese and a Swahili
  phrase) and two were judged right as written. In the six texts added later it marked sixteen: fourteen were changed
  (among them a Finnish idiom that said "came visiting" where the text meant "the village", an ungrammatical Filipino
  question, a Persian word that read as "no longer", and four grammar notes that misquoted their own text) and two were
  judged right as written. Both times it reported nothing in most of those languages (27 and 19 of the 32). A second model finding nothing
  is weaker evidence than a native reader finding nothing.
- **Not checked**: a physical microphone, how the voices sound to a native ear, Google sign-in, and phones. The texts and phrases were written by an AI and no native speaker
  has read them.
